## Question 1

What is the primary purpose of "Sequence Diagrams" in the runtime view?

- [ ] To show the static structure of a system.
- [x] To show how objects interact in a particular sequence of time, modeling interactions in a use
  case.
- [ ] To depict the internal structure of a class.
- [ ] To illustrate the deployment of software components.

<details>
<summary>Rationale</summary>
<p>

**Rationale:** Sequence diagrams show time-sequenced object interactions for use case modeling.
Static structure (Class diagrams), class internals (Composite Structure), and deployment (Deployment
diagrams) are separate UML diagram purposes.

</p>
</details>

## Question 2

What do "Communication Diagrams" focus on?

- [ ] The chronological order of events.
- [x] The interactions between objects and their structural organization concerning the sending and
  receiving of messages.
- [ ] The states of an object over its lifetime.
- [ ] The flow of control in a business process.

<details>
<summary>Rationale</summary>
<p>

**Rationale:** Communication diagrams focus on object interactions and structural message
organization. Chronological order (Sequence diagrams), object states (State diagrams), and process
flow (Activity diagrams) are separate behavioral modeling concerns.

</p>
</details>

## Question 3

What is the purpose of "Interaction Overview Diagrams"?

- [ ] To show simple, linear interactions.
- [x] To combine elements of activity and sequence diagrams to show control flow with nodes that can
  contain interaction diagrams, useful for modeling complex interactions.
- [ ] To depict the static relationships between classes.
- [ ] To illustrate the deployment of software on hardware.

<details>
<summary>Rationale</summary>
<p>

**Rationale:** Interaction Overview diagrams combine Activity and Sequence elements for complex
interaction control flow. Simple interactions, static relationships (Class diagrams), and
deployment (Deployment diagrams) are separate concerns.

</p>
</details>

## Question 4

What is a common drawback of Sequence Diagrams, despite their utility?

- [ ] They are too simple to convey meaningful information.
- [x] They quickly become complex and often can only depict a small portion of a use case with a
  limited number of objects involved.
- [ ] They only show static relationships, not dynamic behavior.
- [ ] They are rarely used in practice.

<details>
<summary>Rationale</summary>
<p>

**Rationale:** Sequence diagrams quickly become complex, limiting use case portions and object
numbers. They're not too simple, do show dynamic behavior (not static), and are widely
used—complexity is the limitation.

</p>
</details>

## Question 5

In a Sequence Diagram, how are method invocations and returns typically represented?

- [ ] Both are represented by dashed arrows.
- [x] Method invocations are represented by solid arrows, and returns are shown as dashed arrows.
- [ ] Both are represented by solid arrows.
- [ ] Method invocations are represented by dashed arrows, and returns are shown as solid arrows.

<details>
<summary>Rationale</summary>
<p>

**Rationale:** Sequence diagrams use solid arrows for method invocations and dashed arrows for
returns. Both-dashed, both-solid, and reversed conventions all violate standard UML sequence diagram
notation.

</p>
</details>
